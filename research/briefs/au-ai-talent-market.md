## 1. Topic & question

**Topic**: The Australian AI and machine learning talent market, and the hiring
pressures acting on it in financial services.

**Research question**:
> What can be defensibly said, from primary sources, about AI/ML hiring in Australia in 2025–2026?
> Specifically: (a) what public salary data actually exists and how reliable is it; (b) is there a
> measurable "AI skills shortage" and how does it relate to the broader tech market; (c) what is
> driving AI governance and risk hiring in APRA-regulated entities; (d) what does the evidence say
> about AI's effect on Australian employment so far.
> Time horizon: data from 2025 to August 2026.

**Audience for the resulting deliverable**: Heads of Talent Acquisition, CDOs and technology
executives in Australian banks, insurers and superannuation funds — readers who will check a number.

---

## 2. Executive thesis

The phrase "AI talent shortage" is doing two contradictory jobs in the Australian market at once.
Measured broadly, tech hiring has *loosened*: Australia's technology workforce contracted 0.3% in
2025 to roughly 967,000 people — the first decline on record¹ — and Jobs and Skills Australia
removed software engineer from the national shortage list entirely, in every state and territory².
Measured narrowly, AI-specific roles have tightened sharply: AI Engineer was the fastest-growing
job title in Australia on LinkedIn's 2026 Jobs on the Rise list, at roughly 150% growth³.
Separately, and independently of market conditions, APRA's 30 April 2026 letter to industry created
a compliance-driven demand shock for AI governance and assurance skills that most entities had not
budgeted for⁴. The honest summary is that Australia does not have a general AI hiring crisis — it
has a severe scarcity in a narrow band of roles, sitting inside a tech market that is otherwise
easier to hire in than it was two years ago.

¹ stat_tech_workforce_decline · ² stat_softeng_no_shortage · ³ stat_ai_engineer_growth · ⁴ stat_apra_letter

## 4. Market sizing — low / base / high

Not a market-sizing question. The nearest quantified equivalent is the AI/ML compensation band,
where no tier-1 or tier-2 source publishes Australian figures at all.

| Estimate | Value | Source | Methodology |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | ~$115,000 base (junior/mid ML engineer) | Specialist recruiter guides | Self-reported placement ranges; not audited |
| Base | ~$137,500 average (ML engineer, all levels) | Glassdoor | Self-reported employee submissions |
| Base (AI engineer) | ~$190,000 typical | Hays | Recruiter placement data; commercially interested |
| High | $250,000 base (AI engineer, upper bound) | Hays | Recruiter placement data; commercially interested |

**All four are tier 3–4.** They mix advertised salaries, self-reported salaries and recruiter
placement data — three incompatible methodologies — and inconsistently include superannuation.
Per the number rules, these must not be combined into a derived "consensus" figure.

## 5. Competing perspectives

- **Shortage vs. no shortage.** Jobs and Skills Australia removed software engineer from the
  shortage list in every state and ICT demand fell 49%², while LinkedIn ranks AI Engineer the
  fastest-growing role in the country at ~150% growth³. These are not actually contradictory —
  JSA measures ANZSCO occupations, which do not have a granular "AI engineer" code, so AI-specific
  demand is invisible in the government data and gets absorbed into broader software categories.
  **This definitional gap is the single most important thing in this brief.**
- **Workforce contraction vs. employment growth.** ACS/Deloitte reports the tech workforce shrank
  0.3% in 2025¹; the Office of the Chief Economist reports software development employment up 25%
  since Nov 2022¹³. Different bases (whole ICT workforce including sales/trades/admin, vs.
  software development occupations) and different windows.
- **AI as job destroyer.** Popular commentary vs. the Office of the Chief Economist, which found
  AI-exposed occupations *grew* 5.6% and no evidence of broad upheaval. The government data is the
  stronger source here.

## 6. White space

- ⚠ **AI/ML salary data specific to Australian financial services** — nobody publishes it. Not
  Hays, not SEEK, not JSA. Any article claiming sector-specific bands is extrapolating.
- ⚠ **How many AI governance/model risk professionals exist in Australia** — no credible count.
  Do not publish a number like "nine candidates nationally" as fact.
- ⚠ **Post-APRA-letter hiring response** — the letter is four months old; no one has measured the
  hiring reaction yet.
- ⚠ **Whether AI-role premiums are stable or a bubble** — only vendor/recruiter data exists.

## 7. Risks & caveats (top 5)

| Risk | Description | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | All salary figures are tier 3–4 with incompatible methodologies; publishing them as authoritative benchmarks is indefensible | SEEK/Glassdoor/Hays methodology notes |
| 2 | Hays is a direct competitor publishing salary data it has a commercial interest in; cite as "per Hays", never as market fact | stat_hays_ai_salary |
| 3 | ANZSCO occupation codes lag AI job titles, so government shortage data systematically understates AI-specific scarcity — cuts both ways in an argument | stat_softeng_no_shortage |
| 4 | APRA explicitly did **not** introduce new requirements; writing "APRA now requires X" would misstate the regulator | stat_apra_no_new_rules |
| 5 | LinkedIn growth percentages start from a small base; ~150% growth in a tiny category is not the same as absolute volume | stat_ai_engineer_growth |
